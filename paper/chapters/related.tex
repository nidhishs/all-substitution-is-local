\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Boundary regret sits at the intersection of several literatures: residual expertise, deferral and abstention, realized collaboration, and decision-theoretic information value.

\paragraph{Residual Human Expertise Audits.}
Residual-expertise audits ask whether human judgments retain predictive information that an algorithm has not already captured.
\citet{AlurEtAl2023} test for residual expertise using conditional covariance, log-loss improvement, and conditional-independence checks. \citet{AlurRaghavanShah2024} give an algorithmic-indistinguishability account of human expertise in prediction.
\citet{KleinbergEtAl2018} formalize the selective-labels problem: when human labels are observed only on reviewed cases, estimating how outcomes depend on both the instance and the human judgment requires accounting for non-random review.
\citet{GuoEtAl2025} decompose information value at the feature level via ILIV-SHAP, attributing each input feature's contribution to the agent-complementary VoI of an AI prediction.
These works focus on whether humans have residual predictive information, or which features drive it. Boundary regret asks a different question: does that information change the reward-optimal action?
Residual expertise can exist without any residual decision value, and ILIV-SHAP's feature-level decomposition does not reveal whether those features' predictive contributions cross a reward-induced decision facet.

\paragraph{Deferral, Selective Prediction, and Reject Options.}
Learning-to-defer methods train systems to choose between acting automatically and handing an instance to a human expert \citep{MadraseEtAl2018,MozannarSontag2020,HemmerEtAl2023,MaoMohri2023}.
Selective-prediction and reject-option methods similarly optimize when a classifier should abstain under a specified loss \citep{RamaswamyEtAl2018,FrancPrusa2023}, and cost-sensitive variants incorporate asymmetric penalties into the deferral objective \citep{AlvesEtAl2024}.
These methods ask when to defer or abstain given a loss, an expert channel, and a collaboration protocol.
Boundary regret measures something earlier in the stack: whether the human signal can move the deployed action across a reward-induced boundary, before any protocol is chosen.

\paragraph{Realized Human--AI Collaboration.}
Work on realized human--AI collaboration asks when teams actually achieve complementarity.
\citet{Vaccaro2024} synthesize evidence that human--AI combinations often fail to outperform the better standalone agent. \citet{SchoefferEtAl2024} show that explanations can change reliance without necessarily helping users identify correct advice.
\citet{McLaughlinSpiess2024} model recommendation design for realized complementarity, and \citet{HemmerEtAl2025} organize evidence on when complementarity appears in human--AI collaboration.
Related critiques emphasize that complementarity also depends on action-set design, evaluation metrics, and interaction design \citep{StraitouriEtAl2025}.
These works ask whether a deployed team realizes value. Boundary regret asks a more local question: is reward-local value already present in the joint distribution, before behavioral frictions, interfaces, and incentives decide whether it shows up?

\paragraph{Value of Information, Decision Geometry, and Decision-Focused Learning.}
The closest conceptual lineage is value-of-information theory and decision geometry.
\citet{Blackwell1951} orders information structures by decision value. \citet{RadnerStiglitz1984} and \citet{DeLaraGossner2020} characterize when information value vanishes as posteriors remain inside action regions, and \citet{KamenicaGentzkow2011} expresses information value through convex value functions and induced decision regions.
Decision-focused learning makes the adjacent algorithmic point that prediction should be trained for downstream decisions rather than generic accuracy \citep{Elmachtoub2022,Wilder2019}, and decision calibration asks when predictions are indistinguishable to downstream best-response rules \citep{Zhao2021}.
In clinical statistics, decision curve analysis \citep{Vickers2006} evaluates predictive models under threshold-encoded reward rather than generic accuracy, plotting net benefit across the full range of clinician decision thresholds; this established the principle that model utility should be assessed under its downstream loss.
Those literatures ask how information, predictions, or training losses affect decisions in general, or how individual models perform under reward.
Boundary regret turns that geometry into a human--AI audit. It is not a new deferral algorithm or a behavioral model of teamwork. It is a reward-local check on whether the human signal has decision value for the action that is actually being deployed --- the first such audit grounded in a Lean-formalized VoI identity.
