\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

The experiments are sequential.
Experiment~1 validates the boundary-regret estimator in synthetic settings where the ground truth is computable in closed form.
Experiment~2 tests whether residual predictive expertise and residual decision value diverge on a real human--AI dataset across reward structures.
Experiment~3 asks whether an ex ante boundary-regret score can allocate scarce human review more efficiently than standard baselines under asymmetric rewards, validated on both synthetic and real data.

\subsection{Experiment 1: Estimator and Geometry Validation}
\label{sec:exp1}

\paragraph{Question.}
Can the boundary-regret estimator recover known $\BR$ values and reproduce the theorem's geometry under controlled finite-action Bayesian decision problems?

\paragraph{Setup.}
We simulate finite-state, finite-action Bayesian decision problems.
For each problem we specify a state space of size $K$, an action set of size $|A|$, a reward matrix $R\in\R^{|A|\times K}$, a model channel $M$ with likelihood kernel $P(m\mid y)$, a human channel $H$ with likelihood kernel $P(h\mid y)$, and a prior $p(y)$ over states.
We assume conditional independence: $M \perp H \mid Y$.

For each instance the generative model samples a true state $y\sim p(y)$, a model signal $m\sim P(m\mid y)$, and a human signal $h\sim P(h\mid y)$.
The model belief is the exact posterior $b_m=P(y\mid m)$ (playing the role of $b_x$ in the main-text notation, with the model signal $m$ serving as the instance index), the augmented belief is the joint posterior $b_{m,h}=P(y\mid m,h) \propto P(m\mid y)\,P(h\mid y)\,p(y)$, the model-only action is $a_m=\argmax_a r_a\cdot b_m$, and the instance-level boundary regret is
\[
  \BR(m,h) \;=\; V(b_{m,h}) - r_{a_m}\cdot b_{m,h}.
\]
Because the channel parameters are known, we can compute exact $\BR$ for every instance.
The \emph{estimator} instead learns the channel parameters from finite training data and computes $\widehat\BR$ from the learned posteriors; comparing $\widehat\BR$ to the exact $\BR$ validates measurement, not the theorem.

\paragraph{Estimation pipeline.}
We draw $N=10{,}000$ instances per condition.
A 5-fold cross-fitting scheme splits instances into folds; for each held-out fold the channel likelihoods $\hat P(m\mid y)$ and $\hat P(h\mid y)$ and the prior $\hat p(y)$ are estimated from the remaining four folds using frequency counts with Laplace smoothing.
The estimated model belief $\bpost_m = P(y\mid m;\hat P)$ and the estimated augmented belief $\bpost_{m,h} = P(y\mid m,h;\hat P)$ are then computed via Bayes' rule for each held-out instance, and $\widehat\BR$ is the plug-in boundary regret at $\bpost_{m,h}$ relative to the estimated model-only action.
Cross-fitting ensures that no instance appears in the training data used to form its own posterior estimates.

To test calibration sensitivity, we apply temperature scaling to the estimated posteriors: $\bpost^{(T)}\propto\exp\bigl(\log\bpost/T\bigr)$ for $T\in\{0.5, 0.8, 1.0, 1.2, 2.0\}$.
Values $T<1$ simulate overconfident calibration (posteriors pushed toward corners); $T>1$ simulates underconfident calibration (posteriors pushed toward uniform).

\paragraph{Conditions.}
We run five conditions, each designed to isolate a specific property of the boundary-regret estimand.
Every condition is tested across six $(K,|A|)$ configurations---$(3,2)$, $(3,3)$, $(5,3)$, $(5,5)$, $(8,3)$, $(8,5)$---for 30 runs total.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em,labelindent=0em]
\item[C1: Predictive signal, no boundary crossing.]
  $H$ is a weakly informative channel, and the prior is concentrated deep inside one action's decision region (action~0 has reward 10 in state~0 vs.\ $\le 2$ elsewhere; the prior places 50\% mass on state~0).
  $H$ shifts beliefs slightly but cannot reach any decision boundary.
  \emph{Expected:} $\BR\approx 0$ for nearly all instances (at higher $K$ and $|A|$, a small fraction of posteriors may drift past a boundary).

\item[C2: Human signal crosses a decision boundary.]
  Each action specializes in one state ($r_a=5$ on state $a\bmod K$, else $1$).
  The prior is near-uniform with a slight state-0 bias, placing model beliefs near the boundary between action~0 and action~1.
  $H$ is a moderately strong channel (diagonal 0.7) that pushes some posteriors across the boundary.
  \emph{Expected:} $\BR>0$ for instances where $H$ crosses the boundary; the estimator should rank these correctly.

\item[C3: High log-loss gain, zero boundary regret.]
  This is the central claim in synthetic form.
  A ``safe'' action pays $19$ in every state; specialized actions pay $20$ in their target state and $0$ elsewhere.
  A specialized action beats safe only when $b_k > 19/20 = 0.95$.
  $M$ is very weak (diagonal 0.05; beliefs $\approx$ prior), and $H$ is strong (diagonal 0.85), concentrating posteriors to ${\sim}0.90$ on the correct state.
  The combined posterior cannot clear the 0.95 threshold.
  \emph{Expected:} Large log-loss gain (beliefs move from ${\sim}1/K$ to ${\sim}0.90$), but $\BR=0$ everywhere.

\item[C4: Same-boundary substitutability.]
  Both $M$ and $H$ distinguish state~0 from all others ($M$: 0.80 vs.\ 0.20; $H$: 0.75 vs.\ 0.25).
  They answer the same question and are informationally redundant.
  \emph{Expected:} $\Delta\VoI(H\mid M,b) < 0$ (substitutes), where $\Delta\VoI(H \mid M, b) := \E_{o_M}[\VoI(H \mid \hat{b}_{M,o_M})] - \VoI(H \mid b)$ measures the change in VoI of the human channel when the model channel has already been observed, averaged over model posteriors (see Appendix~\ref{app:asil}).

\item[C5: Different-boundary complementarity.]
  We use the reward matrix $R=\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}12&0&3\\0&12&3\\3&3&9\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$ from the ASIL formalization, with prior $b=(1/4,\,1/6,\,7/12)$ in the verified complement region.
  $M$ distinguishes state~1 (likelihoods 0.75 vs.\ 0.25), and $H$ distinguishes state~2 (likelihoods 0.75 vs.\ 0.25), crossing \emph{different} decision boundaries.
  For $K>3$ or $|A|>3$, extra states receive neutral reward and extra actions are safe-like; the prior places ${\sim}8\%$ mass on background states.
  For $|A|=2$, the ASIL theorem guarantees complementarity is impossible: with two actions there is a single decision boundary, and any two channels are weakly substitutable.
  \emph{Expected:} $\Delta\VoI>0$ for $|A|\ge 3$; $\Delta\VoI\le 0$ for $|A|=2$.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Metrics.}
For each run we report:
(i) Spearman rank correlation $\rho(\widehat\BR,\BR)$;
(ii) false-positive rate (FPR): fraction of instances with $\BR=0$ where $\widehat\BR>0$;
(iii) expected calibration error (ECE) between $\widehat\BR$ and $\BR$ in 10 bins;
(iv) Spearman correlation between log-loss gain and true $\BR$, written $\rho(\text{LL},\BR)$;
(v) for C4 and C5, the sign of $\Delta\VoI(H\mid M,b)$.

\paragraph{Verdicts.}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.8em]
\item \textbf{C1 + C3 (true $\BR\approx 0$): FPR\,$=$\,0.000.}
  When the human signal has negligible decision value (C1: 2\% of instances have small positive true $\BR$, concentrated at $K\!=\!5,\,|A|\!=\!5$; C3: exactly 0\%), the estimator produces no false positives. This is the safety-critical direction for deployment: the system will not waste review budget on zero-value cases.

\item \textbf{C2 (boundary crossing): $\rho = 0.769$.}
  When boundary regret varies, the estimator ranks instances accurately. At $K\!=\!3,\,|A|\!=\!2$ the correlation reaches $0.999$; it degrades gracefully as the state space grows ($K\!=\!8$: $\rho\approx 0.44$--$0.77$), consistent with increased posterior estimation difficulty.

\item \textbf{C3 (expertise $\neq$ decision value): \%BR$>$0\,$=$\,0.0\%, $\rho(\text{LL},\BR)=0.077$.}
  $H$ is maximally informative (posteriors shift from ${\sim}1/K$ to ${\sim}0.90$ on the correct state) yet has zero decision value in all 60{,}000 instances across six configurations.
  Log-loss gain is large and positive, but boundary regret is identically zero because no posterior exceeds the $0.95$ threshold.
  This is the synthetic instantiation of the paper's central claim: residual predictive expertise and residual decision value can fully decouple.

\item \textbf{C4 (substitutability): $\Delta\VoI<0$ in all configurations.}
  When both channels cross the same decision boundary, they carry redundant information and are substitutes. The sign is correct in all six $(K,|A|)$ configurations.
  However, the estimator has elevated FPR in C4 (up to $0.324$ at $K\!=\!5,\,|A|\!=\!5$; average $0.165$), because finite-sample estimation noise near the zero-BR boundary creates spurious boundary crossings.
  This is an estimation artifact, not a theoretical failure: the true $\BR$ is exactly zero in all C4 instances, but the estimated posteriors occasionally land just past a facet.

\item \textbf{C5 (complementarity): $\Delta\VoI>0$ for $|A|\ge 3$; $\Delta\VoI\le 0$ for $|A|=2$.}
  When $M$ and $H$ cross \emph{different} decision boundaries, the information is complementary.
  At $K\!=\!3,\,|A|\!=\!3$ the measured $\Delta\VoI=0.078=5/64$, exactly matching the Lean-verified ASIL value.
  For $|A|=2$, complementarity is impossible (a single boundary offers no room for different crossings), confirming the ASIL theorem that two-action problems force substitutability.
\end{enumerate}

The key asymmetry: overconfident miscalibration ($T=0.5$) is the dangerous direction.
Under C3, FPR rises from $0.000$ at $T=1.0$ to $0.393$ at $T=0.5$: overconfident posteriors push beliefs past the decision threshold, creating phantom boundary crossings.

\subsection{Experiment 2: Residual Expertise Does Not Equal Decision Value}
\label{sec:exp2}

\paragraph{Question.}
On a real human--AI dataset, how often does a standard expertise audit find residual signal that carries negligible decision value?

\paragraph{Dataset.}
CheXpert public test set \citep{Irvin2019}: 500 frontal chest X-ray studies.
Four binary detection tasks pass a $\ge 20$-positive prevalence gate for all five readers: Cardiomegaly, Edema, Atelectasis, and Pleural Effusion (Pneumothorax excluded: the most prolific radiologist labeled at most 14 positives).

\paragraph{Human signal.}
Each of five board-certified radiologists $\{\text{bc1, bc2, bc3, bc5, bc7}\}$ serves as $h$ in turn; the remaining four define the ground truth (below).
This yields $5 \times 4 = 20$ (reader, condition) pairs.
We present aggregate results over all 20 pairs and use bc1/Cardiomegaly as a detailed exemplar.

\paragraph{Ground truth.}
For each reader $h$, $y$ = majority vote of the remaining four radiologists ($\ge\!2$ of 4 $\to$ positive).
This rotating leave-one-out design eliminates $h$--$y$ contamination by construction.

\paragraph{Model belief.}
$b_x = P(Y\!=\!1\mid x)$ from TorchXRayVision \texttt{densenet121-res224-chex}, with condition-specific output and temperature scaling optimized per (condition, reader) pair on a 150-instance calibration split ($T_{\mathrm{opt}}$ range: $1.01$--$3.79$ across pairs; MCE range: $0.28$--$0.49$).
The sensitivity analysis below confirms the main findings are robust across this calibration range.

\paragraph{Augmented belief.}
$\bpost_{x,h} = P(Y\!=\!1\mid x,h)$ is estimated per pair by 5-fold cross-fitted logistic regression on features $[\operatorname{logit}(b_x),\,h]$.
Cross-fitting ensures each evaluation instance's prediction comes from a model that never saw that instance.
Unlike Experiment~1, the model and human here share the same image $x$, so $M \not\perp H \mid Y$; the logistic regression on $[\operatorname{logit}(b_x),\,h]$ absorbs this dependence directly, and the theorems themselves make no conditional-independence assumption.

\paragraph{Reward matrices.}
Three binary reward matrices, chosen before running the experiment (actions: $a\!=\!0$ discharge, $a\!=\!1$ admit):
\begin{align}
  \text{R1} &= \begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}, &
  \text{threshold } b &= 0.5 & \text{(symmetric accuracy)};
  \label{eq:R1} \\[4pt]
  \text{R2} &= \begin{bmatrix}1&0\\-4&1\end{bmatrix}, &
  \text{threshold } b &= \tfrac{5}{6}\approx 0.833 & \text{(FP penalty: admitting a negative costs 4)};
  \label{eq:R2} \\[4pt]
  \text{R3} &= \begin{bmatrix}1&-4\\0&1\end{bmatrix}, &
  \text{threshold } b &= \tfrac{1}{6}\approx 0.167 & \text{(FN penalty: discharging a positive costs 4)}.
  \label{eq:R3}
\end{align}
In the bc1/Cardiomegaly exemplar, under R2 the model admits only 13.4\% of instances ($b_x>0.833$); under R3 the model admits 87.7\%.

\paragraph{Audits.}
For each evaluation instance we compute:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Residual expertise (log-loss gain):}
    $\ell(x,h) = \log p(y\mid x,h) - \log p(y\mid x)$.
  \item \emph{Boundary regret:}
    $\widehat\BR(x,h) = V(\bpost_{x,h}) - r_{a_x}\cdot\bpost_{x,h}$,
    where $a_x = \argmax_a r_a\cdot b_x$.
  \item \emph{Facet distance:}
    $|b_x - b^*|$, where $b^*$ is the reward-induced decision threshold.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Aggregate results across 20 pairs.}
Under R2 (FP penalty), the Spearman correlation between log-loss gain and $\widehat\BR$ is \emph{negative} in all 20 (reader, condition) pairs (mean $\rho = -0.183$, range $[-0.297,\, -0.024]$): across readers and conditions, high-information human labels cluster at intermediate probabilities well below the $0.833$ threshold, so the posterior update does not change the action.
Under R1 (symmetric), the correlation is \emph{positive} in all 20 pairs (range $[+0.190,\, +0.522]$), consistent with the theory: when the decision boundary is at $b^*=0.5$, the region where readers are most informative overlaps with the region where actions change.
Among top-expertise-decile instances under R2, a mean of \textbf{95.9\%} have $\widehat\BR = 0$ across the 20 pairs.
The mean facet-distance correlation is $-0.343$ under R2, confirming the theorem's geometric prediction: boundary regret tracks proximity to the reward-induced facet, not predictive expertise.
The separation replicates across all five readers and all four conditions, ruling out reader-specific or condition-specific confounds.

\paragraph{Residual expertise is real.}
In the exemplar pair, bc1 is genuinely informative: log-loss gain is positive for 78\% of evaluation instances, with median 0.18 and mean 0.12.
Across all 20 pairs, every reader shows substantial residual predictive information beyond the DenseNet.

\paragraph{Decision value depends on the reward.}
Under symmetric reward (R1), log-loss gain and boundary regret are positively correlated ($\rho=0.309$, $p<10^{-8}$): when the decision threshold is at $b^*=0.5$, the region of the belief space where bc1 is informative ($b_x\approx 0.4$--$0.7$) overlaps with the region where actions change.
Under the false-positive penalty (R2), the correlation \emph{flips negative} ($\rho=-0.110$, $p=0.04$): bc1's highest-information labels cluster at intermediate probabilities, well below R2's threshold of 0.833.
bc1 improves probabilistic predictions on these cases, but the posterior update cannot clear the high-confidence-to-admit threshold --- the action is unchanged.
Under the false-negative penalty (R3, threshold 0.167), the model already admits 87.7\% of instances; bc1's informative positive labels land on already-admitted cases and change no actions ($\rho=0.025$, $p=0.65$).

\paragraph{Top-expertise cases have near-zero decision value under R2.}
In the bc1/Cardiomegaly exemplar, \textbf{97.1\%} of top-expertise-decile instances have $\widehat\BR=0$ under R2, and 100\% under R3.
The aggregate across all 20 pairs is \textbf{95.9\%} under R2.
Under R1, 37.1\% of the same instances have zero boundary regret in the exemplar.
The same human signal, the same instances, but the decision value changes entirely with the reward structure.

\paragraph{Boundary regret tracks facet distance, not expertise (exemplar).}
In the bc1/Cardiomegaly pair, the Spearman correlation between facet distance ($|b_x - b^*|$) and $\widehat\BR$ is strongly negative across all three reward matrices ($\rho=-0.45$ to $-0.58$, all $p<10^{-15}$); the aggregate across 20 pairs under R2 is $\rho=-0.343$.
Instances with high $\widehat\BR$ are those where $b_x$ is close to the reward-induced decision boundary, regardless of how informative the reader is.
This confirms the theorem's geometric prediction: boundary regret is governed by proximity to the reward-induced facet, not by predictive expertise.

\paragraph{Reward-matrix sensitivity (exemplar).}
Among the top 70\% of bc1/Cardiomegaly instances by log-loss gain, 65.7\% have positive boundary regret under R1 but exactly zero boundary regret under R2.
The same human signal on the same instance changes the action under symmetric reward and has no decision value under the false-positive penalty.

\paragraph{Sensitivity analysis (exemplar).}
Recomputing $\rho(\widehat\BR,\ell(x,h))$ under R2 with uncalibrated model beliefs $b_{x,\mathrm{raw}}$ (no temperature scaling) yields $\rho=-0.073$ (same sign, same verdict).
The separation between predictive expertise and decision value is robust to calibration quality; the consistent pattern across all 20 pairs at varying MCE levels ($0.28$--$0.49$) corroborates this.

\paragraph{Caveats.}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item The DenseNet was trained on CheXpert train/valid; minimal leakage into the held-out test set cannot be ruled out entirely.
\item The R2 and R3 cost structures are generic asymmetric penalties, not condition-specific clinical cost-effectiveness values.
\item MCE ranges $0.28$--$0.49$ across pairs.
  The sensitivity analysis on bc1/Cardiomegaly (uncalibrated $b_{x,\mathrm{raw}}$: $\rho=-0.073$, same sign) and the consistent pattern across all 20 pairs confirm robustness to calibration quality.
\item Model margin ($|b_x - b^*|$) correlates more strongly with $\widehat\BR$ than log-loss gain does under R2.
  The distinction between margin and boundary regret is that the latter incorporates the estimated strength of the human signal; Experiment~3 separates them empirically.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experiment 3: Review Allocation under Scarce Human Input}
\label{sec:exp3}

\paragraph{Question.}
When human review is scarce, can an ex ante boundary-regret score identify the cases where obtaining human input would most improve the deployed decision?

\paragraph{Setup.}
We construct a review allocation task from synthetic binary-state, binary-action Bayesian decision problems.
$N = 2{,}000$ instances are drawn with a moderately informative model channel and a stronger human channel, using the same generative process and 5-fold cross-fitting as Experiment~1.
A review policy selects a fraction $q \in \{5\%,\, 10\%,\, 20\%,\, 50\%\}$ of instances for human review using only information available \emph{before} review.
Reviewed instances take the human-updated action $\argmax_a r_a \cdot \bpost_{x,h}$; unreviewed instances keep the model-only action $a_x$.
Utility is evaluated on held-out outcomes under the same three reward matrices as Experiment~2: R1 (symmetric), R2 (FP penalty, threshold $0.833$), and R3 (FN penalty, threshold $0.167$).

The ex ante boundary-regret score is
$\widehat\BR_{\mathrm{pre}}(x) = \widehat\E_{h \mid x}[V(\bpost_{x,h}) - r_{a_x} \cdot \bpost_{x,h}]$,
estimated from training folds as follows: a logistic regression models $P(h \mid x)$ using the same feature set as the model channel; for each possible human signal value $h$, the cross-fitted augmented posterior $\bpost_{x,h}$ is computed; the expectation is taken over $h$ weighted by $\hat P(h \mid x)$.
The evaluation instance's realized $h$ is not used in scoring.

\paragraph{Policies.}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em,labelindent=0em,nosep]
\item[BR\textsubscript{pre}:] review where $\widehat\BR_{\mathrm{pre}}(x)$ is largest.
\item[Entropy:] review where the Shannon entropy $H(b_x)$ is largest.
\item[Margin:] review where the reward-specific margin $|r_{a_x} \cdot b_x - \max_{a' \neq a_x} r_{a'} \cdot b_x|$ is smallest (i.e., $b_x$ is closest to the decision facet under $R$).
\item[Residual:] review where the estimated log-loss gain from observing $H$ is largest.
\item[L2D:] jointly trained deferral classifier (logistic regression on model features, trained to predict whether review improves accuracy under the symmetric loss).
  We note that this baseline is trained on accuracy, not on the reward-specific utility; under asymmetric rewards, this puts L2D at a disadvantage analogous to entropy.
  A reward-aware L2D variant would be a stronger baseline; we leave this to future work and interpret L2D results accordingly.
\item[Random:] uniform random selection.
\item[Oracle:] selects exactly the instances with hindsight-positive utility gain (upper bound, not deployable).
\end{description}

\paragraph{Under symmetric rewards, all policies are comparable.}
When the decision boundary coincides with the maximum-entropy region (R1, threshold $0.5$), entropy, margin, and boundary-regret allocation select similar cases.
BR\textsubscript{pre} leads by a small margin ($0.029$ vs.\ $0.028$ at $q\!=\!10\%$), consistent with the theory: under symmetric accuracy, the reward-induced facet lies where uncertainty is already highest.

\paragraph{Under asymmetric rewards, boundary regret dominates reward-agnostic baselines.}
The story changes sharply under R2 (threshold $0.833$) and R3 (threshold $0.167$), where the decision boundary diverges from the maximum-entropy region.
Entropy and residual-expertise baselines produce \emph{zero} utility gain at both $q\!=\!10\%$ and $q\!=\!20\%$: they select instances near $b_x \approx 0.5$, far from the reward-relevant facet, where no human signal can cross the distant decision threshold.
Margin performs better than entropy because it targets the reward-specific facet, but BR\textsubscript{pre} outperforms margin by a factor of $2\times$ under R2 and $2.6\times$ under R3 at $q\!=\!10\%$.
The additional gain comes from boundary regret's second ingredient: it selects cases not only near the facet but where the human signal is estimated to be strong enough to cross it.

\paragraph{Gain per review.}
At $q\!=\!10\%$ under R2, each reviewed case selected by BR\textsubscript{pre} yields $0.60$ utility points on average, compared to $0.30$ for margin and $0.00$ for entropy.
Under R3, the efficiency is $0.65$ for BR\textsubscript{pre} vs.\ $0.24$ for margin and $0.00$ for entropy.
BR\textsubscript{pre} achieves 58--64\% of the oracle's efficiency at $q\!=\!10\%$.

\paragraph{Budget-utility curve.}
At $q\!=\!5\%$ (results in Appendix~\ref{app:exp3-full}), the gaps widen further: BR\textsubscript{pre} gains $0.033$ under R2 while entropy gains zero.
At $q\!=\!50\%$, the advantages diminish as the budget is sufficient to cover most decision-relevant cases regardless of allocation strategy; all non-trivial policies approach oracle performance.
The key regime is $q \leq 20\%$---where human review is genuinely scarce and allocation policy matters most---and this is exactly where BR\textsubscript{pre} provides the largest advantage.

\paragraph{Why entropy fails under asymmetric rewards.}
The mechanism is the one predicted by Theorem~\ref{thm:facet-bridge}: under R2, the decision boundary is at $b^* = 0.833$; entropy is maximized at $b_x = 0.5$, which is $0.33$ from the boundary.
Reviewing cases near $b_x = 0.5$ improves probabilistic predictions, but even a strong human signal cannot push $b_{x,h}$ from $0.5$ past $0.833$---so the action is unchanged.
This mechanism operates identically in the synthetic and real-data settings: entropy achieves zero utility gain under R2 in both Tables~\ref{tab:exp3} and~\ref{tab:exp3-real}, confirming that the allocation-level failure of entropy under asymmetric rewards is a consequence of the reward geometry, not a dataset-specific artifact.
